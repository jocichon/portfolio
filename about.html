Who I am

I'm Jo Cichon, a software engineer and researcher interested in the intersection of technology, ethics, and human decision-making.

My academic background spans philosophy, mathematics, software development, and computer science. As an undergraduate, I pursued bachelor's degrees in both philosophy and mathematics, convinced that the boundary between the humanities and sciences was largely artificial. The intellectual history connecting thinkers such as Descartes, Leibniz, Russell, Gödel, and Turing reinforced my belief that neither discipline could be fully understood in isolation. After exploring different career paths that never quite fit, I returned to higher education, earning a Bachelor of Applied Science in Software Development from Green River College and a Master of Science in Computer Science from Northeastern University's Khoury College of Computer Sciences, concentrating in artificial intelligence and data science. During my graduate studies, a Human-Computer Interaction course introduced me to Paul Dourish's work on phenomenology and embodied interaction, reconnecting my technical education with the philosophical questions that had shaped my earliest academic interests. That connection ultimately led to my current research in responsible AI. My education has taught me to approach technology as more than a collection of technical problems. I want to understand not only how systems function, but why we build them as we do, how human assumptions and decisions become embedded in their design, and what responsibilities follow.

Research

My research interests center on responsible AI, particularly the relationship between technical development, organizational decision-making, and ethical accountability.

I'm currently collaborating with Dr. Brianna Dym on Mirror or Self-Portrait, a study of how AI ethics scholarship frames ethical responsibility. Our work examines whether ethical concerns are treated as properties to be measured and managed or as questions that require ongoing interpretation, reflection, and human judgment.

I'm particularly interested in what happens when ethical concerns emerge during development. Who has the authority to raise them? How are they evaluated? And what happens when addressing them conflicts with the pressure to deliver?

These questions inform my broader interest in how organizations can make ethical accountability an integral part of engineering practice rather than something deferred until after deployment.

Writing

I write about software engineering, artificial intelligence, and the sometimes uncomfortable questions that arise when technological capability outpaces our willingness to examine its consequences.

My writing draws connections between academic research, engineering practice, and everyday experience. I'm especially interested in the assumptions we inherit, the shortcuts we normalize, and the ethical debt we accumulate when difficult questions are postponed in the interest of progress.
